# Hadron UX research: human factors, accessibility, and AI reliance

Research date: 26 October 2026. Scope: Hadron's GitHub pull-request review dashboard, repository setup, review findings, and human review queue.

This register contains **50 distinct sources whose pages were opened and whose relevant text or author abstracts were inspected**. It is one half of the 100-source research set. Research abstracts support only the limited findings recorded below; this is not a systematic literature review or a claim that every paper was read in full. The application column is our design inference for Hadron, not an experimental result about this product.

Evidence labels distinguish **guidance** from **research**. NN/g guidance draws on practitioner experience and usability research; it is not 20 independent controlled experiments. W3C Understanding documents explain accessibility success criteria; APG provides implementation patterns. Research findings are conditional on their studied tasks and participants. Company design guidance does not prove that every product from a company implements it.

## Decisions this evidence supports

1. **Make the next review action obvious.** Lead with pull requests, their actual status, findings, and items awaiting human review. Keep repository setup accessible. Put operational detail behind clear secondary navigation. Sources: H01–H04, H09, H16, H19.
2. **Use truthful reassurance.** A completed review is not a guarantee of secure code or a safe deployment. Show the recorded recommendation, distinguish pending from failed, and preserve the user's access to evidence. Remove fabricated health percentages, invented time savings, title-based business-impact claims, and controls that only pretend to approve a review. Sources: H05, H43–H50.
3. **Make explanations easy to check.** Pair a finding with its file, location, rationale, severity, and suggested change when available. Do not replace evidence with reassuring analogies. Keep technical detail discoverable without requiring a separate persona mode. Sources: H02–H04, H42, H44, H46, H49–H50.
4. **Reserve friction for consequential actions.** Reading, searching, and navigating should be quick. Before a persistent review decision, name the effect and target; report success only after the server confirms it. Avoid fake urgency or repeated blanket confirmations. Sources: H06, H08, H20, H43, H45.
5. **Use calm hierarchy and familiar controls.** Prefer a consistent type scale, aligned rows, restrained borders, readable secondary text, and one clear primary action per local task. These are design choices to test, not universal psychological laws about a specific color or number of cards. Sources: H07, H09, H11–H14, H18–H19, H42.
6. **Treat accessibility as part of the workflow.** Support keyboard operation, visible focus, labeled controls, announced asynchronous state changes, sufficient contrast, responsive layouts, and reduced motion. Test actual interactions instead of assuming ARIA attributes alone make a control accessible. Sources: H21–H40.
7. **Validate outcomes with users.** Observe whether a reviewer can find a pending item, understand uncertainty, inspect evidence, and complete the intended decision. Measure errors and successful task completion alongside speed and perceived clarity. Sources: H17–H18, H43–H44, H46–H50.

## Corrections to the previous principle set

The earlier `principals.md` framed an artificial safety gauge, absolute reassurance, one-click sign-off, and emotional manipulation as design goals. Those instructions should not guide implementation. H43–H50 support a more careful distinction between making evidence understandable and making people believe a system is always right.

The research above does not establish that gradients reduce stress, red universally causes fear, a certain layout triggers dopamine, or “digital vastu” predicts usability. Spatial balance can inform visual composition, but any usability benefit must be checked through accessibility and task-based evaluation. No claim about a user's physiology is needed to justify consistent spacing and a clear hierarchy.

Similarly, do not turn memory-capacity experiments into a hard seven-item menu rule, treat three actions as a scientific maximum, or interpret the absence of findings as proof of security. Hadron needs enough information for informed review, presented in a manageable order.

## Evaluation plan and limits

| Task | Evidence to collect | Acceptance direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First visit | Ask a participant to explain what Hadron does and start setup. | They identify PR review and find repository connection without coaching. |
| Find work | Ask them to locate a pending human decision in a realistic list. | They distinguish pending, completed, and failed reviews and find the relevant item. |
| Assess a finding | Ask what evidence supports the recommendation and what remains uncertain. | They reach file/context evidence and do not interpret approval as a deployment guarantee. |
| Submit a decision | Observe both success and a simulated network failure. | No duplicate submission, no false success, entered text retained, useful recovery offered. |
| Keyboard and assistive technology | Navigate, filter, open/close dialogs, inspect details, and submit. | Focus remains visible and predictable; controls and status changes are announced appropriately. |
| Narrow screens and enlargement | Test representative mobile sizes, 200% text enlargement, and 320-CSS-pixel reflow. | Essential content and actions remain available without page-level horizontal overflow. |

These are proposed research and verification criteria, not claims that participant studies have already occurred. Automated checks and developer inspection can establish specific technical behavior; they cannot establish that users feel less fear, that the product is universally intuitive, or that all production risks are resolved. The implementation report should record which checks actually ran and identify remaining live-service or user-study work.
